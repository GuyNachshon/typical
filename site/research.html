Research

Typical reads the decision out of the model before it emits a letter, instead of generating an answer and parsing it back. The negative result matters just as much: a candidate-blind state carries priors and calibration, not question-conditioned knowledge (§3j, §3s). Below is the full architecture, data, pipeline, and result set, including the numbers that didn't move.

Section references (§…) point to the project's internal REPORT.md, which isn't public yet. The Hugging Face model cards reproduce the tables cited here.

Architecture

A frozen Qwen3 trunk, cut at roughly 71% depth, feeds a small contextual readout with LoRA on the top 8 kept layers and a factored ∅ head. The state is read once and cached. Each query appends a short rendered suffix to that cache. Nothing is generated: the label, the yes/no, and the level all come out of the same terminal decision state.

a) Why mid-depth, not the last layer

The project's first version (a candidate-blind decision vector, frozen 0.6B backbone) read the trunk's last layer and built a hypothesis-only model: SNLI accuracy landed at 58.6%, exactly what a model gets from the hypothesis alone, ignoring the premise (REPORT §2.4). Moving the tap to layer 20 of 28 and adding LoRA fixed it: 65.3% frozen, 68.2% with LoRA. The premise finally mattered.

The same question came back for the native, candidate-aware readout: is full depth (28 of 28) better once the candidates are inside the computation too? It isn't. Matched runs at layer 28 vs. layer 20 (same N3 readout, same data, same steps) put tap 20 ahead on every evidence task: SNLI .906 vs. .863, MNLI .865 vs. .752, ANLI .519 vs. .431, BoolQ .834 vs. .737 (REPORT §3r), while keeping the question-dependent signal (Δ_q .118 vs. .117 at 28 layers). Depth isn't free capacity here: for a candidate-aware readout, the last third of the trunk loses evidence reasoning instead of accumulating it.

b) Why there's no rendered "none of the above" line

An earlier version rendered ∅ as a literal candidate in the suffix, the way a multiple-choice prompt adds "E. none of the above." That line was the failure mode: null probability collapsed to always-abstain at large label sets (K=150 CLINC, K=77 Banking77) and never-abstain at small ones (K=5), with MMLU-Pro false-abstain at .692.

Making ∅ a separate head decision instead (letters_nonull plus a factored null) fixed the K-sweep: smooth and monotone at every K. MMLU-Pro false-abstain dropped to .003, Banking77-77 accuracy went from .063 (unusable) to .579, Δ_q held at .122, and NLI/BoolQ cost nothing (REPORT §3t). The remaining cost: topic/intent label spaces lose 2–7 points (HWU64 .801 → .757, 20NG .589 → .515), and choice-set null probes lose some AUROC. A real trade, not a free win.

c) Why the candidates have to be inside the computation

A separate line of experiments tried to compile a listwise teacher's knowledge into a candidate-blind decision state Z(x, q), computed before the candidate set is known, so one state could in principle serve any question. A choices-only control (state = ".", labels only) showed why that fails: every student architecture tested (a single vector, 8 probes, 8 probes with set-conditioning, under both single-set and same-Z multi-set distillation with an explicit Δ-log-odds objective) scored the same or better with the real question replaced by a shuffled one. None of the above-chance accuracy was question-dependent. All of it was candidate-set priors and calibration, learned uniformly across every head shape (REPORT §3j).

Re-running the same student at full depth (28 layers, not just tap 20) closed the question: Δ_q stayed at −0.008, within noise of zero, while evidence tasks regressed exactly as the depth result above predicts (REPORT §3s). A candidate-blind state compiles priors and calibration. Question-conditioned knowledge doesn't, at any tap. That's why Typical renders its candidates (Choice's label set, Score's levels) into the suffix and reads them in the same forward pass as the question, instead of looking them up afterward against a fixed state.

Data

E .40 / K .15 / W .35 / U .10. The workflow corpora are ours and programmatic. Held-out families, grammars, styles, and an untrained level-7 tier measure transfer, not memorisation.

Family weights and corpus row counts (releases/typical-small.md, Training args + Data section). kb 147,446 rows (MMLU-aux/AQuA/MedMCQA/LogiQA-style distillation) · wf 72,000 rows / 12 families, our rubric-conditioned workflow corpus · wf_hf 86,600 rows from three HF-sourced workflow sets · wh 60,605 rows, DecisionMix v2 · u 31,029 rows, soft-target uncertainty corpus.

DecisionMix v2 (bucket W, data_wh)

A programmatic rule engine (scripts/decisionmix_v2.py): 12 domains × 6 boolean conditions, rendered at levels 1–7, with 100% of training rows drawn in counterfactual rubric groups (same state and candidates, at least two distinct golds across the group: same facts, different rule, different answer). Held out entirely from training: one domain/level pair (wh_heldout_grammar), three whole domains (wh_heldout_family), two rubric styles (wh_heldout_style), and level 7 (temporal, numeric, probability, and trade-off composition), which is eval-only and never trained on. A leak audit against JevBench's 231 public items found 0/231 hits.

The uncertainty corpus (bucket U, data_u)

31,029 soft-target training rows, same generator family. Real sources: UNLI's validation split (train/test are consumed elsewhere in the mix) and the ambiguous rows of metaeval/ambient (uniform target over the listed labels). metaeval/chaos-mnli-ambiguity is eval-only, never trained on. The remaining ~92% comes from four synthetic generators with exact closed-form targets: partial evidence, a noisy-sensor Bayes posterior, ordinal confusion, and conflicting-sources log-odds, each with a held-out parameter range. Also 0/231 JevBench leak hits.

Null augmentation

--null_aug W:0.20: 15–20% of workflow rows have their gold candidate removed at train time, so the target becomes ∅. This teaches abstention on rows that would otherwise always have an answer. Combined with the factored-∅ head design (see Architecture above), it keeps the null calibrated across K instead of collapsing at the extremes.

Findings

Eight established results. Each has a matched control and, where it mattered, a seed pair or a full-row re-evaluation (REPORT §6).

D1 — JevBench: public subset (231 ids), unranked, n = 72 standard (SE ≈ .058); probabilities conditioned on non-∅; hard tier at chance for both models.

  1. Candidate-blind states carry priors and calibration, not question-dependent knowledge. Holds at tap 20 and at full depth. A candidate-aware suffix computation keeps the knowledge. A direct contextual readout keeps it too, with a quarter of the letter interface's order fragility. (REPORT §3j, §3l, §3s, §3v)

  2. The evidence-vs-knowledge trade-off was two artifacts: last-layer tap and a rendered "none of the above" line. Removing both gives one model (nc_v3_tap20, research checkpoint, not released): evidence at energy level, Δ_q kept, null monotone in K, JevBench standard .694 with no workflow training. (REPORT §3r, §3t)

  3. Rubric-conditioned workflow data teaches the decision shapes it contains, not general external abstraction. Held-out rubric styles +34, held-out families +9–14, rubric-flip both-correct .43 → .57. jevlogs, PagerDuty, and Mind2Web stayed at their constant-prediction floor for K-way Choice until the typed heads and DecisionMix v2 arrived (findings 6–7 below). (REPORT §3w, §3x)

  4. Mixing is a first-class variable, not a detail. E .45–.50 restores evidence. ∅-augmented W rows fix abstention and MMLU, where a single eval-time null threshold can't serve both E and W at once. The exact W fraction in [.1, .2] doesn't matter once null augmentation is on. (REPORT §3y, §3z, §3aa)

  5. Small batch = under-fitting = better hard-tier and soft-target numbers, at the cost of everything in-distribution. At 1.7B, the JevBench hard tier is a probability-quality problem, not a data-volume one. (REPORT §3aa)

  6. Typed primitives: ordinal-smoothed Score and a Bernoulli Noul head both win on probability quality, at zero or negative decision-accuracy cost. Ordinal smoothing (τ=0.7) changes no JevBench decision while held-out score NLL goes 2.07 → 1.23 (intermediate checkpoints, §3ac; released models' held-out score NLL: 1.01 / 1.02). The Bernoulli Noul head is exactly order-invariant by construction and is the first thing to clear an untouched external floor by a margin: PagerDuty .602 → .886 (intermediate checkpoint, §3ac; released models .817 / .838 vs floor .792). (REPORT §3ac)

  7. DecisionMix v2's hard curriculum transfers within its own rule grammar, not beyond it. Held-out families/grammars +34–37, rubric-flip .48 → .71, JevBench adversarial .33 → .83 (intermediate checkpoints r1_cand → r1_dmv2, §3ad, not released; the released models' equivalent wh_heldout numbers are in the held-out chart below: typical-small .836/.898/.896, typical-medium .874/.893/.891), while level-7 composition (temporal, unit, expected-value, trade-off) sits at ~.50, chance, for every checkpoint on the ladder. The U corpus buys likelihood, not top-1 accuracy. (REPORT §3ad)

  8. Same recipe at scale: 1.7B → 4B → 14B lifts standard-tier accuracy, knowledge, and in-distribution workflow decisions monotonically, at roughly constant latency. JevBench standard .750 → .833 → .875, MMLU-Pro among-K .330 → .457 → .514, held-out noul .70 → .84 → .89, at 45 → 56 → 60 ms per decision (scaling-ladder checkpoints, §3ab, same pre-DecisionMix-v2 recipe at every size, none released; typical-small/typical-medium use a later recipe and score .694 / .806 on JevBench standard). 8B is a checkpoint outlier in both frozen and trained form, not part of the trend. Long-state families and soft-target calibration don't scale: the frozen 14B with three shots beats the trained 14B on the hard tier (.559 vs. .468), so the hard tier is a data/objective problem at every size, not a capacity one. (REPORT §3ab)

Knowledge retained from the backbone

MMLU-Pro among-K, Δ_q (shuffled-question control), and TruthfulQA-MC1 measure how much of the frozen backbone's knowledge survives the fine-tune. They aren't a selling point. typical-small: among-K .343, Δ_q .127, TruthfulQA .257. typical-medium: among-K .458, Δ_q .193, TruthfulQA .408 (REPORT §3af table). Compare to level-7 composition, discussed above, which the training data never touches and which stays at chance regardless of size.

The long-state bug and what comes next

The hard-tier collapse at scale had a specific cause, not a capacity limit, and the fix is training now (REPORT §3ag, 2026-09-21/22; runs in flight).

a) The data bug

data_wf_long (the 23k long-policy rows added in §3z) renders Case: <facts> <request> with the facts last. Measured state lengths are p10/p50/p90 = 1,190 / 1,845 / 2,490 tokens, and training right-truncates at --max_state. At 1,024 tokens (Release 1) that cut the facts off 98.8% of those rows; at 256 tokens (the scaling ladder) it cut all of them off. Every model since §3z was trained to answer long policies confidently from states that no longer contained the facts, which is exactly the long_policy score: .05 for the 14B (§3ab), .21 for typical-medium.

Fixes (commit 2fad326): facts-first regeneration (wf/train_long_v2.jsonl, case position under 8% of the text), --drop_truncated (rows longer than the window are dropped, never cut), --grad_ckpt, --best_on (checkpoint selection on the uncertainty and curriculum val NLL), --brier_lambda, and frozen-backbone teacher labels via scripts/teacher_label.py --zero_shot --shots 3.

b) The mask bug

A second bug (commit 6d0a7e3): the SDPA padding mask was built as long, which forces PyTorch's O(L²) math kernel. That caused the 14B OOMs at 3,072-token states, and the scaling ladder's memory pain generally. Building it as bool fixed it.

d) The Qwen3.5 port

Qwen3.5 port (commit 9b1ffac). Base checkpoints exist at 0.8B / 2B / 4B / 9B / 27B / 35B-A3B, a complete replacement ladder. Differences handled: the VL wrapper config (text_config, .language_model unwrapped), the hybrid stack (3× Gated-DeltaNet + 1× full attention, tap at 71% depth keeps 4 of 6 full-attention layers at 0.8B), LoRA targets (in_proj_qkv/z/b/a and out_proj on DeltaNet layers, q/k/v/o on attention layers, MLP everywhere), and a transformers-5.17 cache gap (LinearAttentionLayer lacks batch_repeat_interleave) patched in native_kv_decide. Cached-vs-full parity is 1e-7, Qwen3 behavior is unchanged (153 tests), and the public inference/ package mirrors the port.

e) In flight

tl1b (Qwen3-14B, facts-first long rows, 3,072-token states, frozen-14B knowledge distillation, 8k steps, best-on calibration val) and its matched control tl1b_nokd; tm2 (the Release-1 recipe on Qwen3.5-4B-Base, 2,048-token states); and the SemIf rendering probe. Pass rule for typical-large: hard ≥ .559 or hard Brier ≤ .65, long_policy ≥ .35, and CLINC-150 / MMLU among-K within 2 points of ladder_14b.

Limitations

Re-derived for typical-small and typical-medium specifically, not inherited from any prior write-up.

  • We do not claim to be faster, better, or cheaper than Jev or any hosted system. There's no apples-to-apples run, and JevBench is unranked for us.
  • We do not do vision, audio, real-time control, game-playing skill, or planning/lookahead. Text-only, one forward pass, no search.
  • We are not a knowledge model and do not reason: MMLU-Pro sits at .34 / .46 among-K, and level-7 composition (temporal, units, expected value, trade-off) is at chance (.495 / .544, floor .46). We do not do arithmetic, timezones, or trade-offs.
  • We do not handle long documents. Trained at ≤1,024-token states, JevBench long_policy is .26 / .21, and the preview checkpoint's hard tier goes from .64 at ≤256 tokens to .23 above 1,024. The cause is known: data_wf_long rendered facts last, and training right-truncated them out at --max_state, so the model learned to answer long policies from states that had already lost the facts (REPORT §3ag). A fix (facts-first regeneration, --drop_truncated) is training now, not yet in a release.
  • We do not claim "any label set, any domain." CLINC is −4 versus the untrained base; TREC-fine is .508 / .414 (typical-medium is −10 versus its own untyped control); held-out rubric styles regress −4 / −6.
  • We do not claim exact order-invariance for Choice, or exact independence of irrelevant alternatives. Choice is order-robust (mean-max Δp .01–.08), not order-invariant; only Noul is exact. Adding an irrelevant candidate moves Δlog-odds .09–.22.
  • We do not claim out-of-distribution calibration or hard-tier competence. Both released models are at chance on the JevBench hard tier, with Brier .79 / .77.
  • We do not ship 3 open sizes. There are two backbone sizes (1.7B, 4B) and three checkpoints (typical-small-preview is also 1.7B). There is no typical-large; the 14B point is a scaling-ladder measurement, not a release.
  • Training code is not released. Only inference/ is public today; training code release to follow.
  • We do not claim JevBench standard improved from typical-small to the preview. It went .750 → .694 (~1 SE), traded for calibration and the typed heads.
  • We do not natively serve very large candidate sets (hundreds to thousands) at this head; that needs an energy → top-r → native path, not shipped yet. The native suffix budget is 1,024 tokens by default.
  • We do not claim to beat supervised classifiers on CLINC, Banking77, or TREC.
  • jevlogs numbers are not for commercial use (research-licensed, block-level labels, held-out sanity eval only). The licenses of metaeval/ambient and metaeval/chaos-mnli-ambiguity are undeclared on their HF cards; flagged here, not asserted.

Reproduce

pip install -r inference/requirements.txt

from typical import Typical  # inference/typical/, downloaded alongside your code

m = Typical.from_pretrained("OzLabs/typical-small", device="auto")

# K-way choice over a fixed label set -> {label: p, ...} + p_null
m.choice(state, "What does the customer want?", ["refund", "replacement", "repair"])

# Yes/no -> P(yes)
m.noul(state, "Is the order still under warranty?")

# Ordinal levels -> {level: p, ...} + p_null + expected (E[index])
m.score(state, "How urgent is this ticket?", ["0", "1", "2", "3"])
