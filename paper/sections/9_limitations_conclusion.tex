\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Scope.} Text-only, bounded decisions over candidate sets that fit in a short suffix. No
planning, search, tool execution, or vision. The typed primitives cover categorical, ordinal, and
Bernoulli contracts and nothing else.

\paragraph{Generalization.} ``Runtime-defined labels'' means the axes of \Cref{tab:generalization}, not
arbitrary unseen domains or ontologies. Transfer is strong on unseen label wordings and unseen label
spaces inside trained domains, mixed on external workflow sets (several sit at or below their own
majority-class floor for every checkpoint, which measures the set as much as the model), and absent on
unseen composition grammar. Our rubric-conditioned generalization results share one rule engine between
the training generator and the held-out families, so they are an evaluation-protocol result rather than a
claim about general compositional rule execution.

\paragraph{Computation.} Hard compositional decisions are unresolved at every size tested. Large
candidate sets require a shortlisting front end. Per-query cost scales with candidate tokens, so the
cached-state saving is real but bounded.

\paragraph{Probability quality.} Calibration does not hold uniformly: not on the hard tier, not
out-of-distribution, and not across decision types under one global knob. We report it as an axis, never
as a property of the system.

\paragraph{Interface artifacts.} Choice retains order sensitivity. The exact-odds property of
\Cref{eq:factored-null} holds for pairwise non-null candidates under the factored softmax only -- the
null gate is set-dependent by design, so the model as a whole is not independent of irrelevant
alternatives. Part of the measured order robustness comes from per-step option shuffling during
training, not from architecture alone.

\paragraph{Statistical power.} Outside the explicit two-seed comparisons of \Cref{sec:repr:readout},
every number in this paper is a single training run; noise-floor measurements elsewhere in this project
put NLI/BoolQ variation at $\pm0.7$ points and held-out label-space variation at $\pm3$--5 points between
identically configured seeds. JevBench intervals are $\pm6$--13 points (standard, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}36$)
and $\pm9$ points (hard, $n{=}111$). Several differences we report, including the frozen-teacher
distillation control and the JevBench-scored truncation ablation, are inside those intervals and are
reported as such.
The 1.7B/4B/8B/14B ladder is entirely within the Qwen3 family, with a single Qwen3.5-4B checkpoint as a
second-family data point, not a second ladder; Qwen3-8B is an outlier in both frozen and trained form and
we did not find a configuration that recovers it within budget. Two checkpoints were still training at
the time of writing and are omitted rather than reported provisionally, and a Qwen3.5-9B checkpoint has
hard-tier results only. We also note the converse of low power: neighbouring rungs of the ladder are not
separable on JevBench's hard tier at $n{=}111$ (\Cref{sec:train:ladder}), so the ladder's value is its
trend, not any pairwise ordering within it. Where a question mattered enough, the fix was to build an
evaluation set large enough to answer it rather than to argue about the benchmark
(\Cref{sec:boundary:truncation}).

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Every JevBench number is the public 231-item subset and is not a ranked
leaderboard entry; the 146 judge-scored items required for ranking are not public, so we cannot rule out
that our public-subset numbers differ systematically from a full-benchmark ranking. Rendering and
protocol move scores by more than the differences between many entries
(\Cref{sec:boundary:rendering}), which is why we treat cross-entry comparison as context
(\Cref{app:leaderboard}) rather than evidence. Several evaluation-only external sets rely on rule-based
or model-assisted gold labels that we did not independently re-verify. Latency numbers come from a
single-stream Python harness with no optimized-runtime or saturated-server baseline.

\paragraph{The released checkpoints carry the defect this paper diagnoses.} Both public models were
trained before the facts-first corpus fix, so they inherit its positional bias, and a reader who
downloads them inherits it too. On the 605-item held-out long-state set, moving the case block from
after the policy body to before it costs \texttt{typical-small} 20.0 points (.798 $\to$ .598) and
\texttt{typical-medium} 25.5 (.866 $\to$ .612), with \texttt{typical-small} landing on the
majority-class floor of the yes/no family. Since the caller writes the state text, the operational
consequence is a rendering instruction --- evidence last --- and the correct fix is retraining on the
corrected corpus, which \Cref{sec:boundary:truncation} values at 8--11 points and which we have not
yet done for the released sizes. We state this rather than deferring it to the model cards because it
is the same mechanism the paper reports, observed in the artifacts the paper ships.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and licensing.} Inference code, the released checkpoints, and every run
manifest (harness commit, checkpoint hash, dependency lock) are public. Training code and several
generated corpora carry licence constraints from their upstream sources; \Cref{app:corpora} records
provenance and licence per corpus, and \Cref{app:ops} records what is and is not redistributable.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether a causal language model can be used as a direct typed decision engine without
generating an answer token, and what has to remain inside its computation for that to work. The central
result is that the most reusable formulation -- a candidate-blind decision state, computed before the
candidate set is known -- fails to preserve question-conditioned closed-book capability under the
factorizations, objectives, and depths we tested, and fails in a way that raw accuracy and calibration
both conceal. Keeping the candidates inside the causal computation and reading their contextual states
against a cached state prefix recovers that signal, at roughly a quarter of the letter interface's order
sensitivity, and supports typed categorical, ordinal, Bernoulli, and abstention outputs with no answer
token emitted.

The result is not that generation is unnecessary in general. The same fine-tuning that improves standard
bounded decisions does not solve hard compositional ones, and on that tier a same-size frozen backbone
read with three exemplars is not beaten by anything we trained. Along the way we found that rendering --
where the evidence sits, how the options are written -- moves a frozen model's benchmark score by more
than the architectural differences it is usually used to adjudicate, which is a reason to control it
before comparing decision architectures at all.

That leaves a sharper question than the one we started with: how much computation must occur between a
state and a decision, and when can it remain non-generative?
