\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Typical makes text-only decisions over candidate sets that fit in a short suffix; sets in the hundreds
go through a shortlisting front end (\Cref{sec:results:cost}). Multi-step composition is the main open
problem: on DecisionMix level 7 (never trained) the v1 1.7B and 4B models score .495 and .544 against a
.441 guess rate and the 14B model .620, and on the JevBench hard tier a frozen 14B backbone with three
exemplars beats our trained 14B (paired $+.108$ $[+.027, +.189]$, $p = .015$; \Cref{app:hard-tier}). A
fixed-depth pass has limited serial computation \citep{merrill2023parallelism}, which intermediate
tokens extend \citep{merrill2024expressive,feng2023chain}. Formatting is the one factor in
\Cref{tab:dependence} that scale reduces without removing (6.8--7.6 points at 14B), and Choice is
order-robust rather than order-invariant. The readout and evidence-first results are replicated across
seeds; other rows are single runs, JevBench numbers use its 231 public items (unranked), and
rubric-generalization results share one rule engine between training and held-out families. The
training code is not yet released.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Typical reads decisions straight out of a pretrained language model. Given a document, a question and
options supplied at request time, it returns a probability for each option and for abstaining, with no
generated text, in 45\,ms at 1.7B and 60\,ms at 14B.

The central lesson is that such a model is only as good as what its decision depends on, and accuracy
does not tell you. We tested each input and factor directly. In every configuration we tested, the decision carried
question-dependent knowledge only when the candidates were read inside the forward pass: six designs that compute the
decision state before seeing them all stayed within .03 of zero on $\dq$. It depends on the evidence when the head reads at
71\% of depth, and on long documents when training places the evidence first (+11.2 points). It depends
on the rubric when training uses counterfactual rubric groups (+35--40 points on held-out rule
families). A Bernoulli head makes yes/no answers independent of label order, and removing the rendered ``none of the above'' option, leaving abstention to the gate, cuts MMLU-Pro
false abstention from .692 to .003. Formatting is the one factor we reduced without removing: a trained
model still depends on the layout it was trained in, less so at 14B.

The tests themselves, $\dq$ and render-crossed evaluation, apply to any system that scores candidates,
and we recommend reporting them alongside accuracy. The 1.7B and 4B models are public with an inference
package. Multi-step composition is the open problem, and adding a small amount of intermediate
computation before the typed readout is the natural next step.
