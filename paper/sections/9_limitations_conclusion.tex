\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Scope.} Typical makes text-only decisions over candidate sets that fit in a short suffix. It
does not plan, search, call tools or read images, and per-query cost grows with the rendered candidate
tokens, so sets in the hundreds go through a shortlisting front end (\Cref{sec:results:cost}).

\paragraph{Multi-step composition.} Decisions that carry an intermediate value through several steps
remain the main open problem. On DecisionMix level 7 (temporal, numeric, expected-value and trade-off
composition, never trained) the v1 1.7B and 4B models score .495 and .544 against a .441 guess rate,
and the 14B model .620. On the JevBench hard tier a frozen 14B backbone prompted with three exemplars
scores above our trained 14B checkpoint (paired difference $+.108$ $[+.027, +.189]$, $p = .015$), while
the trained model leads on the standard tier (\Cref{app:hard-tier}). A single fixed-depth pass has
limited serial computation \citep{merrill2023parallelism}, and intermediate tokens extend it
\citep{merrill2024expressive,feng2023chain}.

\paragraph{Evidence.} The readout comparison and the evidence-first result are replicated across seeds;
other rows are single runs. JevBench numbers use its 231 public items and are not a ranked leaderboard
entry, with intervals of $\pm6$--13 points (standard) and $\pm9$ (hard), so comparisons between our own
checkpoints rest on paired tests and on larger purpose-built sets. Rubric-generalization results share one
rule engine between training and held-out families. Choice is order-robust rather than order-invariant;
only Noul is invariant by construction. The ladder rows come from the recipe current when each was
trained (\Cref{tab:headline}). The training code is not yet released.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Typical shows that a pretrained language model can return a decision directly. Given a document, a
question and options supplied at request time, it returns a probability for each option and for
abstaining, read from hidden states with no generated text.

Three findings make this work. First, the candidates have to be read inside the model's forward pass: in
every configuration we tested, a representation computed before the model saw them stayed within .03 of
zero on $\dq$, against .09--.12 for readouts over the candidates. Second, the head should score each candidate's in-context
representation at about 71\% of depth, which matches the standard answer-letter readout on question-dependent
knowledge ($\dq$ .106 vs.\ .109) with a quarter of its sensitivity to option edits. Third, abstention belongs in a separate gate
outside the options.

Training data shapes a decision model as much as its architecture. Counterfactual rubric groups add
35--40 points on held-out rule families, and placing evidence first in long training documents adds 11.2
points on 605 held-out documents scored with training and evaluation formats crossed.

The resulting models are fast and open. A decision takes 45\,ms at 1.7B and 60\,ms at 14B, the 14B model
reaches .931 on the JevBench standard tier, and the 1.7B and 4B models ship with an inference package.
Multi-step composition is the open problem, and adding a small amount of intermediate computation before
the typed readout is the natural next step.
