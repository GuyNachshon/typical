\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All checkpoints share the architecture fixed in \Cref{sec:analysis}.

\subsection{Typed output contracts}
\label{sec:results:typed}

Each output type delivers the property its contract names. Matched fine-tunes from one parent
checkpoint, each trained for 3k steps on the rows of its own type, are compared against a control that
treats the same rows as plain Choice (\Cref{app:typed-primitives}).

\paragraph{Score.} The ordinal-smoothed target ($\tauord = 0.7$) adds no parameters and improves every
probability-quality measure on held-out urgency at flat accuracy (.505 $\to$ .508): NLL 2.07 $\to$ 1.23,
Brier .79 $\to$ .68, ECE .35 $\to$ .19, ordinal MAE .58 $\to$ .55. On JevBench's 12 ordinal items the two
models make identical per-item predictions, so the gain is in how mass is spread over adjacent levels.

\paragraph{Noul.} The Bernoulli head renders no candidates, so label order cannot enter its computation.
Reversing the labels of a two-way Choice over \{no, yes\} moves $P(\text{yes})$ by up to .55; for rows
routed to the Bernoulli head it moves by exactly zero on all nine test sets. The head is better or
equal on six of seven yes/no sets and clears the untouched PagerDuty floor by a wide margin: accuracy
.602 $\to$ .886 against a .792 majority floor, NLL 1.04 $\to$ .28.

\subsection{The training distribution}
\label{sec:results:mixture}

Decision-type balance, counterfactual rubric groups and null targets each move a separate axis. All
comparisons here are matched 1.7B runs that differ only in data or sampler.

\paragraph{Family balance.} Adding workflow data at family weights E .35 / K .25 / W .40 cost 11 points on
CLINC-150 and 10 on TREC-fine, almost all of it false abstention (CLINC-150 .05 $\to$ .17). The release
sampler, E .40 with U .10, keeps every evidence set within about 1 point of the evidence-only base
(\Cref{app:mixture}).

\paragraph{Counterfactual rubric groups.} Training on DecisionMix v2 (\Cref{sec:train:rubric}) lifts
held-out families, grammars and rubric styles by 35--40 points over a model never trained on the
generator (.481/.507/.491 $\to$ .827/.881/.888, together with the uncertainty corpus), and accuracy on
held-out rubric-flip pairs from .477 to .710; the model now depends on the rubric it is given
(\Cref{app:decisionmix}).

\paragraph{Null augmentation.} Duplicating 20\% of workflow rows with the gold candidate removed and target
$\nullsym$ lowers false abstention on CLINC-150 from .10 to .08 and on TREC-fine from .27 to .12, and
raises accuracy by 2.6, 3.6, 4.3 and 6.0 points on CLINC-150, TREC-fine, HWU64 and 20NG. MMLU-Pro
among-$\numcands$ returns to the evidence-only base (.353), with the highest $\dqsh$ of any mixture
(.143). The model now spends mass on $\nullsym$ where soft gold has none, so held-out Noul drops 4 points
and held-out Score NLL rises (1.52 $\to$ 2.04).

\paragraph{Probability quality.}
\label{sec:results:calibration}
Probability quality is indexed by decision type, so the training data, the target and checkpoint
selection set it; a global temperature and null-logit offset fit on held-out soft-target rows want
opposite corrections for an evidence-and-knowledge and a workflow-trained checkpoint
(\Cref{app:calibration-fit}). On the 14B backbone the recipe bundle (facts-first long states with
truncated rows dropped, a 3{,}072-token window, DecisionMix v2 and the uncertainty corpus, typed heads
with the ordinal target, checkpoint selection on held-out uncertainty and curriculum NLL, and
frozen-teacher distillation) takes held-out Score NLL from 2.87 under the earlier workflow recipe to 0.95,
typed-decisions NLL from 1.96 to 1.04, and JevBench hard-tier Brier from .85 to .66
(\Cref{fig:calibration}). No component is isolated except distillation, and the arm without it reaches
the same Score NLL. The v1 1.7B and 4B checkpoints, which have the ordinal target and the uncertainty
corpus but no likelihood-based selection, already reach 1.01 and 1.03; all 1.7B and 4B checkpoints with
typed heads and DecisionMix sit at 0.97--1.08.

Every trained checkpoint beats its frozen backbone on the standard tier, by 16.7, 2.8, 11.1 and
11.1 points at 1.7B (v1), 4B, 8B and 14B; each gap on its own sits inside its interval (paired test at
14B, frozen minus trained: $-.111$ $[-.250, +.014]$, $p = .10$). Question-conditioned
knowledge grows with the backbone: $\dqsh$ rises from .127 at 1.7B to .235 at 14B, 2.3 times the 1.7B
teacher's; the 8B checkpoint is the exception (.093), and sits below the 4B and 14B under one
matched recipe as well (\Cref{app:extra-figures}). Qwen3.5-4B (\texttt{typical-medium} v2) is the strongest point per parameter: .861
standard and .495 hard, above every Qwen3 checkpoint we trained on the hard tier and 9.7 standard-tier
points above its frozen control. \Cref{sec:limitations} discusses the hard tier, where the frozen 14B leads.

\paragraph{Cost.}
\label{sec:results:cost}
Decision latency is nearly flat in backbone size. On the research harness a single decision at
$\numcands{=}2$ costs 45, 56 and 60\,ms at 1.7B, 4B and 14B: an $8\times$ larger backbone is
$1.3\times$ slower, because the cached state and a short suffix dominate (\Cref{app:latency-sweep}). On the serving path a warm decision takes
15.5--17\,ms at 1.7B and 19--21\,ms for the Qwen3-4B model (warm p50, $\numcands{=}2$,
\Cref{fig:serving}); the Qwen3.5-4B model measures 34--46\,ms with reference DeltaNet kernels
(\Cref{app:serving}).

\subsection{Evidence placement and rendering}
\label{sec:results:long}

Training with the evidence placed first adds 11.2 points on held-out long states when training
right-truncates states at 1{,}024 tokens, where facts placed after the policy text were lost in 98.8\% of
long-policy rows (\Cref{sec:train:long}); the gap replicates at a second seed. Two 1.7B arms with byte-identical flags train on the same 23{,}318 rows,
rendered facts-first or facts-last, with truncation active as at release, and are scored under both
renders (\Cref{tab:twobytwo}, \Cref{app:truncation}). The comparison is a \emph{matched-configuration
gap}: each model is scored in the render it was trained on, so the evaluation render changes along with
training.

At 1.7B the matched-configuration gap is $+.112$ $[+.077, +.147]$, $p = 5\times10^{-10}$, and a second

\paragraph{Rendering.}
\label{sec:results:rendering}
Changing only the rendering moves a frozen 9B model 12.5 points: with identical JevBench items,
frozen Qwen3.5 checkpoints and letter-logit readout, a replication of another published system's state
and option layout lifts the standard tier by 1.4, 8.3 and 12.5 points at 2B, 4B and 9B, surviving a paired
per-item test at 4B ($p = .048$) and 9B ($p < .001$); no hard-tier change separates (\Cref{tab:rendering}).
At 1.7B, render mismatch inside one trained model costs more than the training defect: in the
off-diagonal cells of \Cref{tab:twobytwo}, switching the evaluation render costs 14.5 points for the
facts-first model and 19.0 for the facts-last one, which lands on its \texttt{policy\_permit} floor (.615
against .612); at 14B it costs 7.6 and 6.8 points, so the larger backbone tolerates render mismatch
better. Crossing training render with evaluation render separates data effects from compatibility
effects, and the purpose-built 605-item set resolved at $p < 10^{-9}$ what a 19-item benchmark cell could
not. The frozen controls of \Cref{tab:headline} use a letter-logit rendering we did not sweep, so
frozen-versus-trained comparisons carry a rendering factor of the size shown here.
