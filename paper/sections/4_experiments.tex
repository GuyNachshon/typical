\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Corpora}
\label{sec:experiments:data}

Training draws from seven corpora, summarized in \Cref{tab:corpora}: \textbf{\texttt{data\_v5}} (evidence
-- SNLI \citep{bowman2015snli}, MNLI \citep{williams2018mnli}, ANLI \citep{nie2020anli}, BoolQ
\citep{clark2019boolq} -- plus wide-vocabulary classification including CLINC-150 and Banking77/HWU64
\citep{larson2019clinc,casanueva2020banking77}); \textbf{\texttt{data\_kb}} (closed-book multiple-choice
knowledge distilled from nine standard sources, TruthfulQA-MC1 \citep{lin2022truthfulqa} held out, leakage
audited against MMLU-Pro \citep{wang2024mmlupro,hendrycks2021mmlu}); \textbf{\texttt{data\_wf}} and
\textbf{\texttt{data\_wf\_hf}} (our rubric-conditioned workflow generator plus three externally sourced
typed-decision sets); \textbf{\texttt{data\_wf\_long}} (a longer-state, facts-first variant of the
workflow generator, \Cref{sec:method:mixture}); \textbf{\texttt{data\_wh}} (DecisionMix v2, a second
independent rubric-conditioned generator with compositional depth levels 1--7); and
\textbf{\texttt{data\_u}} (a soft-target uncertainty corpus from UNLI \citep{chen2020unli}, AmbiEnt, and
synthetic closed-form generators, with ChaosNLI-ambiguity \citep{nie2020chaosnli} evaluation-only).
Full per-family breakdowns, licenses, and held-out split sizes are in \Cref{app:corpora}. None of the
seven corpora contains any of the 231 public JevBench ids.

DecisionMix v2 deserves one note on its design, because it carries the paper's strongest causal pressure
on rubric conditioning: it emits \emph{counterfactual rubric groups} -- same state, same candidate set,
different rubric, different gold -- so a model cannot solve the task from the state or from candidate
priors alone. The matching limitation belongs in the same breath: DecisionMix teaches transfer inside its
own generated rule grammar. It does not establish general compositional rule execution, and its level-7
composition remains near chance for every model we trained (\Cref{sec:boundary:hard}).

\subsection{What ``held out'' means: a generalization taxonomy}
\label{sec:experiments:heldout}

``Held out'' is used in this literature for at least seven materially different things, and conflating
them is the fastest way to overread a runtime-defined-label result as arbitrary-domain zero-shot
classification. \Cref{tab:generalization} separates the axes we actually measure and states what a
positive result on each does and does not license. Every claim in \Cref{sec:repr,sec:train,sec:boundary}
is tagged to one of these rows.

\textbf{External, evaluation-only sets.} Six sets are never trained on: \textbf{PagerDuty}
(alert-triage yes/no, floor .792), \textbf{jevlogs} (log-triage yes/no, floor .697), \textbf{Mind2Web}
(web-agent action choice, floor .427), \textbf{tree-choice} (taxonomy-routed choice up to
$\numcands{=}320$), \textbf{typed-decisions} (400 cases spanning all three primitive types with full
instructions, criteria, and probabilistic gold), and \textbf{TruthfulQA-MC1}. We report accuracy against
each set's own majority-class floor because several are class-imbalanced enough that a constant
prediction clears a nontrivial accuracy; a model below its floor is worse than guessing the majority
class.

We additionally report \textbf{rubric-flip accuracy} -- for a state and candidate set rendered under two
rubrics with different gold answers, the fraction of pairs where the model gets \emph{both} answers
correct, only possible if the rubric conditions the answer -- and
$\dr = \mathrm{Acc}(\state, r, \cands) - \mathrm{Acc}(\state, r_{\mathrm{shuf}}, \cands)$, comparing
accuracy under the correct rubric against a swapped one, state and candidates fixed.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\dq$}{Delta-q}: separating question dependence from candidate priors}
\label{sec:experiments:deltaq}

\begin{definition}[Question-dependence gap]
\label{def:deltaq}
For a model $f$ and an evaluation set of decisions,
\[
  \dq = \mathrm{Acc}\big(f(\state, \query, \cands)\big) \;-\; \mathrm{Acc}\big(f(\state, \tilde{\query}, \cands)\big),
\]
where $\tilde{\query}$ replaces the real question while the candidate set $\cands$ and the state are held
fixed. We report two variants: a \emph{choices-only} variant ($\tilde{\query}$ is an empty placeholder)
and the stricter \emph{shuffled-question} variant $\dqsh$ ($\tilde{\query}$ is a real but unrelated
question from the same corpus), which is primary throughout, after the choices-only variant was found to
leak residual candidate priors through the degenerate rendering.
\end{definition}

The interpretation is the reason this metric exists:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{High raw accuracy with $\dq \approx 0$} means the model is exploiting candidate-set
  priors or answer-set artifacts. It is not answering the question. Accuracy alone cannot see this.
  \item \textbf{$\dq > 0$} means a question-dependent signal is present, and its magnitude is
  interpretable relative to a reference model measured on the same items.
\end{itemize}

The reference throughout is the listwise teacher's own $\dqsh = .102$ on 1{,}200 MMLU-Pro items: the
teacher's question-dependent component is itself small, so the measurement has roughly nine points of
dynamic range, not twenty-one. We treat differences under $\pm .02$ as indistinguishable from zero;
that band is approximately $1.4$ standard errors of an \emph{unpaired} difference of proportions at
$n{=}1{,}200$ and $p \approx .15$, and a paired comparison on identical items would be tighter still, so
it is a conservative band. This metric is adopted from the choices-only control of
\citet{balepur2024artifacts}, strengthened to a shuffled real question.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:experiments:metrics}

We report \textbf{accuracy} (argmax against gold), \textbf{Brier score}, \textbf{expected calibration
error (ECE)}, and \textbf{negative log-likelihood (NLL)}, against hard or soft gold as the evaluation set
provides. Where a label space is only partially covered by the candidate set at test time, we separately
report \emph{among-$\numcands$} accuracy (argmax restricted to the true candidate set, ignoring $\pnull$)
so null and discrimination failures are not conflated. Rubric-conditioned score and typed external sets
carry soft, non-one-hot gold, so NLL and Brier score are primary there and accuracy is a secondary,
coarser signal. Calibration is reported as a \emph{measured axis per task and type}, never as a blanket
property of the model.

\subsection{JevBench protocol, disclosures, and confidence intervals}
\label{sec:experiments:jevbench}

JevBench (\texttt{fstandhartinger/jevbench} v1.2.1) is a third-party benchmark for typed decision models,
with items typed \texttt{noul} (yes/no), \texttt{choice} ($\numcands=2$--6), and \texttt{score}
(ordinal), each supplying a state, an instruction/rubric, and an exact label set, split into standard,
easy, and hard tiers. It is one evaluation suite among several here, not the foundation of any claim.

\textbf{Public subset, not a ranked entry.} The harness's public release exposes 231 of its items (72
standard, 48 easy, 111 hard); a further 146 judge-scored items are not public, and the harness's own
leaderboard ranks only submissions covering at least 95\% of items including that private set. Every
number we report is computed on the 231 public ids only and is \emph{not} a ranked leaderboard entry.
We carry this disclosure in the caption of every JevBench table and figure rather than in prose alone.
Contextual comparison against other leaderboard entries is deferred to \Cref{app:leaderboard}, because
protocols and rendering differ between entries, several entries have no method paper, the private judge
items are unavailable, and our own rendering control (\Cref{sec:boundary:rendering}) moves scores by
more than the spread being compared. The scientifically useful JevBench result in this paper is the
within-model matched comparison, not a ranking.

\textbf{Confidence intervals and the paraphrase cluster.} The standard tier contains 72 items but only
36 \emph{independent states}: each state appears as two paraphrases sharing a \texttt{group} field.
Resampling items would treat paraphrase pairs as independent and report intervals that are too narrow,
so we \textbf{cluster-bootstrap over \texttt{group}} (20{,}000 resamples); the hard tier has one item per
group and reduces to an ordinary item bootstrap. The resulting 95\% intervals are wide and should
discipline every reading of this benchmark: roughly $\pm6$ to $\pm13$ points at the standard tier
($n_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}36$) and $\pm9$ points at the hard tier ($n{=}111$). Where two of our own
checkpoints are compared on the same items we additionally report a \emph{paired} cluster bootstrap on
the per-item difference, which is the appropriate test and is materially more powerful than comparing
two marginal intervals. We do not treat any unpaired difference smaller than these intervals as a
ranking.

\textbf{Probabilities are conditioned on non-$\nullsym$.} The harness's scoring expects a distribution
over the labeled candidates only; we report $P(\cand{j} \mid \state, \query, \cands, \neg\nullsym)$, i.e.\
$\pnull$ is computed and then dropped and the remaining mass renormalized.

\textbf{Other disclosures}, held constant across every model we report: option order follows the
harness's own label order (a reversed-order control on one checkpoint swung standard accuracy by $\pm 4$
points); latency is measured in-process on one H100, one decision at a time, with model loading excluded
and cold candidate-label embedding included where applicable; and no hosted-API cost applies to any of
our numbers. Every run's harness commit, checkpoint hash, and dependency lock file are recorded in the
run manifest.

\subsection{Hardware and latency measurement}
\label{sec:experiments:hardware}

Training runs on rented NVIDIA H100 GPUs (SXM and NVL variants), bf16, AdamW, effective batch size 64,
8{,}000--12{,}000 steps depending on the run. Latency is measured in-process, single stream, one decision
at a time unless a batch size is stated, with the state's key-value cache warm and model loading excluded.
Numbers compared across backbone sizes in one table are measured on one pod with one PyTorch/CUDA
build; numbers from different pods or builds are marked as such and not compared directly. We keep two
latency stories separate throughout: a controlled research-harness measurement
(\Cref{sec:train:latency}) and an optimized serving path with key-value reuse (\Cref{app:serving}).
