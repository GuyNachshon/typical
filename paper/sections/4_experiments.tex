\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Corpora}
\label{sec:experiments:data}

Training draws from seven corpora, summarized in \Cref{tab:corpora} (full per-family breakdowns in
\Cref{app:corpora}). \textbf{\texttt{data\_v5}} covers evidence tasks (SNLI \citep{bowman2015snli}, MNLI
\citep{williams2018mnli}, ANLI \citep{nie2020anli}, BoolQ \citep{clark2019boolq}, SQuAD-v2-style reading
comprehension) and classification (including CLINC-150 and Banking77/HWU64
\citep{larson2019clinc,casanueva2020banking77}) with a deliberately wide span of label vocabularies at low
rows-per-source, so the model cannot memorize a fixed small set of label sets and must read the rendered
options. \textbf{\texttt{data\_kb}} is a closed-book multiple-choice knowledge corpus distilled from nine
standard sources (ARC, OpenBookQA, CommonsenseQA, SciQ, QASC, LogiQA, AQuA-RAT, MedMCQA,
MMLU-auxiliary-train), with TruthfulQA-MC1 \citep{lin2022truthfulqa} held out entirely and two items
excluded from all MMLU-Pro \citep{wang2024mmlupro,hendrycks2021mmlu} numbers after a leakage audit against
that evaluation set. \textbf{\texttt{data\_wf}} is our own
rubric-conditioned workflow generator, 15 families of Noul/Choice/Score decisions (policy checks,
adequacy, support, fact verification, routing, action selection, extraction, categorical judgment, tool
selection, and four ordinal scales plus urgency); one whole family per type is held out entirely, and 37\%
of rows belong to \emph{rubric groups} -- the same state and candidate set rendered under two or three
different rubrics with different gold answers, built to measure rubric dependence rather than
state/candidate pattern-matching. \textbf{\texttt{data\_wf\_hf}} adds three externally sourced sets in the
same typed shape (\texttt{cua-s1-forms}, MIT; \texttt{systemone-lite-general}, MIT;
\texttt{jev-4b-distill-data}, Apache-2.0), each with its own held-out test/demo/adversarial split never
trained on. \textbf{\texttt{data\_wf\_long}} is a longer-state variant of the workflow generator for
multi-paragraph policy documents, rendered facts-first (\Cref{sec:method:mixture}).
\textbf{\texttt{data\_wh}} is a second, independent rubric-conditioned generator (DecisionMix v2): a
programmatic rule engine over 12 domains and six boolean conditions at seven levels of compositional
depth, where levels 1--6 are trained and level 7 -- compositions requiring temporal, unit-conversion,
expected-value, or trade-off reasoning the generator does not otherwise produce -- is evaluation-only;
every trained row belongs to a counterfactual rubric group, and one domain/level pair, three whole
domains, and two rubric styles are held out. \textbf{\texttt{data\_u}} is a soft-target uncertainty
corpus: the validation split of UNLI \citep{chen2020unli}, the ambiguous rows of AmbiEnt, and four synthetic generators with
exact closed-form posteriors (partial-evidence combination, a noisy-sensor Bayesian update, ordinal
confusion, and conflicting-source log-odds pooling), each with a held-out parameter range;
ChaosNLI-ambiguity \citep{nie2020chaosnli} is evaluation-only. None of the seven corpora contains any of the 231 public JevBench
ids.

\subsection{External, evaluation-only sets}
\label{sec:experiments:external}

Six sets are never trained on and are used only to measure generalization beyond our own generators:
\textbf{PagerDuty} (alert-triage yes/no, 6{,}000 rows, majority floor .792), \textbf{jevlogs} (log-triage
yes/no, 5{,}080 rows, majority floor .697), \textbf{Mind2Web} (web-agent action choice, 1{,}600 rows,
majority floor .427), \textbf{tree-choice} (a taxonomy-routed high-cardinality choice set, up to $K=320$,
720 rows), \textbf{typed-decisions} (400 cases / 2{,}000 decisions spanning all three primitive types
with full instructions, criteria, and probabilistic gold), and \textbf{TruthfulQA-MC1}. We report accuracy
against each set's own majority-class floor because several of these sets are class-imbalanced enough
that a constant prediction clears a nontrivial accuracy (e.g.\ jevlogs is 70\% ``no''); a model below its
floor is worse than guessing the majority class.

\subsection{Held-out design}
\label{sec:experiments:heldout}

Generalization is measured along four largely independent axes, each with its own held-out split rather
than a single random validation set: (i) \emph{whole families} -- an entire workflow family (e.g.\
eligibility, tool selection, urgency) is excluded from \texttt{data\_wf}/\texttt{data\_wh} training and
evaluated only at test time; (ii) \emph{rubric styles} -- the same decision content rendered in a writing
style (e.g.\ terse vs.\ audit-log phrasing) never seen in training; (iii) \emph{rule grammars} -- for
DecisionMix v2, one domain/level combination's surface grammar is withheld while the underlying boolean
logic is trained on other domains; and (iv) \emph{compositional depth (level 7)} -- decisions requiring a
type of reasoning (temporal arithmetic, unit conversion, expected value, multi-attribute trade-off) that
the generator never produces at any trained level. We additionally report \textbf{rubric-flip accuracy}:
for a state and candidate set rendered under two different rubrics with different gold answers, the
fraction of pairs where the model gets \emph{both} rubrics' answers correct, which is only possible if
the rubric conditions the answer rather than the model pattern-matching the state or candidates alone.
A companion diagnostic, $\dr = \mathrm{Acc}(\state, r, \cands) - \mathrm{Acc}(\state, r_{\mathrm{shuf}}, \cands)$,
compares accuracy under the correct rubric against accuracy when the rubric text is swapped for an
unrelated one from a same-type item, holding the state and candidates fixed.

\subsection{JevBench protocol and disclosures}
\label{sec:experiments:jevbench}

JevBench (\texttt{fstandhartinger/jevbench} v1.2.1) is a third-party benchmark for typed decision models,
with items typed \texttt{noul} (yes/no), \texttt{choice} ($K=2$--6), and \texttt{score} (ordinal), each
supplying a state, an instruction/rubric, and an exact label set, split into standard, easy, and hard
tiers. We report results with the following disclosures made explicit, because each materially affects
how the numbers should be read.

\textbf{Public subset, not a ranked entry.} The harness's public release exposes 231 of its items (72
standard, 48 easy, 111 hard); a further 146 judge-scored items are not public. The harness's own
leaderboard ranks only submissions covering at least 95\% of items including the private judge set. Every
number we report is computed on the 231 public ids only and is \emph{not} a ranked leaderboard entry;
comparisons to leaderboard entries in \Cref{sec:results} are drawn from the same public per-item file and
are only valid for that reason.

\textbf{Effective sample size.} With 72 standard items, the majority-class floor is .311 and the standard
error of a proportion at that floor is $\approx .058$ ($n{=}72$); design effects from paraphrase pairs in
the standard split further reduce the effective sample to $n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 36$. We treat any
standard-tier difference between our own checkpoints under roughly 2 points, and any hard-tier difference
under roughly 6 points, as noise rather than a ranking, and we say so explicitly wherever such a
comparison appears.

\textbf{Probabilities are conditioned on non-$\nullsym$.} The harness's scoring expects a distribution
over the labeled candidates only; we report $P(\cand{j} \mid \state, \query, \cands, \neg\nullsym)$, i.e.\
$\pnull$ is computed and then dropped and the remaining mass renormalized, rather than folding $\pnull$
into the reported distribution.

\textbf{Other disclosures}, held constant across every model we report: option order follows the
harness's own label order (a reversed-order control on one checkpoint swung standard accuracy by $\pm 4$
points, larger than the differences between our own model variants); latency is measured in-process on
one H100, one decision at a time, with model loading excluded and cold candidate-label embedding included
where applicable; and no hosted-API cost applies to any of our numbers (we are not a hosted service on
this benchmark). Every run's harness commit, checkpoint hash, and dependency lock file are recorded in
the run manifest.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:experiments:metrics}

We report \textbf{accuracy} (argmax against gold), \textbf{Brier score} (mean squared distance between
the predicted distribution and the one-hot or soft gold), \textbf{expected calibration error (ECE)}
(binned gap between confidence and accuracy), and \textbf{negative log-likelihood (NLL)} under the
predicted distribution, all against either hard or soft gold as the evaluation set provides. Where a
label space is only partially covered by the candidate set at test time (e.g.\ a held-out intent whose
correct label was never seen in training), we separately report \emph{among-K} accuracy (accuracy of the
argmax restricted to the true candidate set, ignoring $\pnull$) so that null-calibration failures and
discrimination failures are not conflated. $\dq$ is the gap between a model's score on a question and its
score with the question replaced by a placeholder or a shuffled, unrelated question (candidate set held
fixed); it measures question-conditioned signal separately from candidate-set priors, and we report both
a choices-only and a stricter shuffled-question variant, the latter adopted as primary after the former
was shown to still admit some candidate-prior leakage (\Cref{sec:method:blind-closure}). $\dr$ and
rubric-flip accuracy are defined in \Cref{sec:experiments:heldout}. Rubric-conditioned score and typed
external sets carry soft, non-one-hot gold, so NLL and Brier score are the primary metrics there and
accuracy is reported alongside as a secondary, coarser signal.

\subsection{Hardware and latency measurement}
\label{sec:experiments:hardware}

Training runs on rented NVIDIA H100 GPUs (SXM and NVL variants, noted where the distinction matters for
latency comparisons), bf16, AdamW, effective batch size 64 (via gradient accumulation where a single
forward pass does not fit), 8{,}000--12{,}000 steps depending on the run. Latency is measured in-process,
single stream, one decision at a time unless a batch size is stated, with the state's key-value cache
warm (encoded once, reused across queries) and model loading excluded; we report single-decision latency
(one query against a freshly cached state) and marginal per-query latency at a stated number of queries
$M$ against one cached state, both as a function of candidate-set size $K$. Where we compare candidate
readouts at different backbone sizes, all numbers in a given table are measured on one pod with one
PyTorch/CUDA build (``apples-to-apples''); numbers drawn from different pods or builds are marked as such
and not compared directly.
