\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{A. Candidate dependence is not merely a scoring inconvenience.} The standard reading of the
cross-encoder/dual-encoder trade-off is a cost-accuracy curve: putting the candidate in the encoder
costs a forward pass and buys some accuracy. \Cref{sec:repr:blind} says something sharper for
question-conditioned closed-book decisions. It is not that a candidate-blind state scores a few points
lower; it is that under every factorization, objective, and depth we tested, its above-chance accuracy
carries \emph{no} question-dependent component at all. The candidates appear to need to participate in
the language model's own computation for that capability to be expressible. Two practical consequences
follow. The reusable-state formulation a systems engineer wants is available for \emph{shortlisting}
(\Cref{sec:train:latency}) -- which does not require question-conditioned capability -- and not for the
decision itself. And any evaluation of a candidate-independent decision architecture should report
$\dq$, or an equivalent, because accuracy and calibration will both look healthy while the question is
being ignored.

\paragraph{B. Direct decision interfaces trade generative flexibility for structural control.} What the
interface buys is concrete: output bounded to a runtime-supplied set, no answer parsing, typed
probability semantics with an explicit abstention event, and state-prefix reuse across many decisions.
What it costs is equally concrete and we found all of it empirically. Candidate tokens must fit in the
suffix, so the per-query cost is not independent of $\numcands$. Choice remains order-sensitive --
reduced to about a quarter of the letter interface's sensitivity, not eliminated
(\Cref{sec:repr:readout}). Large candidate sets need a second stage. And hard serial computation is
unresolved. This is a trade, not a strict improvement, and the paper should not be read as claiming
otherwise.

\paragraph{C. Fine-tuning reshapes capability, not only interface.} It would be convenient if training a
model to emit direct decisions changed only the output format. \Cref{sec:boundary:frozen} says it does
not: the same recipe that improves the standard tier leaves the hard tier behind a same-size frozen
backbone with three exemplars. The lesson generalizes past our architecture -- teaching a model a target
output distribution can improve behavior inside that distribution while degrading capability outside it,
and aggregate in-domain metrics hide this by construction. The counterpart, which is the same coin, is
\Cref{sec:train:mixture}: the distribution of decision types in training is a first-order lever on
capability, comparable in magnitude to the architectural choices this paper is otherwise about.

\paragraph{D. Probability quality is task-conditional.} There is no evidence in this paper for a single
universal ``confidence'' calibration of a decision model. The same global temperature-and-offset fit
wants opposite corrections on an evidence-trained and a workflow-trained checkpoint
(\Cref{sec:train:calibration}); ordinal targets fix Score's likelihood without changing a single
decision; a Bernoulli head fixes an invariance problem that no amount of recalibration would; and
hard-tier calibration stays poor after every fix. Calibration is best treated as a measured axis indexed
by decision type and task family, and pursued at the data and objective level rather than through one
scalar knob at the output.

\paragraph{How much computation must happen between state and decision?} The decision pass computes at
fixed depth with no generated token, so there is no mechanism for a sequence of dependent intermediate
computations. Log-precision transformers of fixed depth are equivalent in expressive power to
constant-depth threshold circuits \citep{merrill2023parallelism}, which provably cannot solve certain
sequential composition problems at any parameter count, while conditioning on self-generated
intermediate tokens provably escapes that class
\citep{merrill2024expressive,feng2023chain}. The families where our hard tier stays at chance -- temporal
arithmetic, unit conversion, expected value, multi-attribute trade-offs -- are exactly the shape this
picture predicts a single constant-depth pass should struggle with. We are careful not to convert that
into an explanation of our result: \Cref{sec:boundary:hard} favors the data-coverage reading, the two
are not exclusive, and the rendering confound of \Cref{sec:boundary:rendering} weakens any causal claim
built on the frozen comparison. What the theory does suggest is where to look next. If the gap is
computational rather than distributional, the fix is an explicit intermediate-computation mechanism --
more passes, or a small amount of scratch work before a final typed readout -- rather than more
parameters or more of the same data. The question this paper leaves sharpened is not whether generation
is necessary in general, but \emph{how much computation must occur between state and decision, and when
it can remain non-generative}.
