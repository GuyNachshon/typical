\section{Analysis and discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Where the hard tier's remaining gap lives}
\label{sec:analysis:hard}

Breaking JevBench's hard tier down by sub-family (\Cref{fig:hard_families}, \Cref{app:extra-figures})
isolates where training helps and where it does not.
Held-out rubric styles, families, and grammars inside our own generators' distributions move sharply under
workflow training (\Cref{sec:results:kd}, \Cref{app:decisionmix}), but none of it transfers to
\emph{level-7} composition -- decisions requiring temporal arithmetic, unit conversion, expected-value
reasoning, or multi-attribute trade-offs -- which sits at chance for every checkpoint trained on this
project's data, including the 14B model that otherwise leads the JevBench standard-tier comparison. This
is not a capacity story: the frozen 14B backbone with three in-context exemplars does no better on these
families. It is a data-coverage story -- our generators produce boolean rule composition up to six levels
deep but never produce the specific compositional operations level-7 and JevBench's
long\_policy/temporal/probability/tradeoff sub-families require, and no model here acquires that
composition from data that does not contain it.

\subsection{Constant-depth computation without intermediate steps}
\label{sec:analysis:depth}

The decision pass of \Cref{sec:method:pass} computes a decision in one forward pass at fixed depth: no
token is generated, so there is no mechanism for the model to perform a sequence of dependent intermediate
computations the way chain-of-thought decoding does. This is a meaningful constraint, not just an
implementation detail. Log-precision transformers of fixed depth are equivalent in expressive power to
constant-depth threshold circuits \citep{merrill2023parallelism}, a complexity class that provably cannot
solve certain sequential composition problems regardless of parameter count, while allowing a transformer
to generate and condition on its own intermediate tokens provably increases its expressive power beyond
this class \citep{merrill2024expressive,feng2023chain}. Level-7 composition and JevBench's
temporal/numeric/probability/trade-off items are exactly the kind of multi-step, order-dependent
composition this theoretical picture predicts a single constant-depth pass should struggle with: each
requires combining several pieces of the state through more intermediate operations than the family's
lower levels, and none of it is masked by within-batch shortcuts because these items are held out or
external. We do not claim this theoretical result \emph{proves} the specific empirical gap in
\Cref{sec:analysis:hard} -- the failure could equally be a data-coverage artifact of our own generators,
which is the reading \Cref{sec:analysis:hard} favors -- but the two readings are not mutually exclusive,
and the theoretical picture predicts that even a data fix would hit a ceiling for compositional depth
that scales with the state, not with any decision the same architecture makes in one constant-depth pass.
It is the natural reason a non-generative decision interface should be expected to underperform a
generative one specifically on compositional, multi-step items, independent of backbone scale -- and a
concrete argument for why the fix, if there is one within this architecture, is more likely to be an
explicit intermediate-computation mechanism (more forward passes, or a small amount of generated
scratch work before the final typed readout) than more parameters or more of the same kind of training
data.

\subsection{Training improves standard while degrading hard, relative to a frozen model with shots}
\label{sec:analysis:tension}

The sharpest empirical tension in this project is that our training recipe, evaluated against the
identical frozen backbone with no training at all, moves JevBench's standard and hard tiers in opposite
directions at scale. At 14B, the frozen backbone with three in-context exemplars scores .559 on the hard
tier and .819 on standard; our best trained 14B checkpoint scores .450 on hard and .931 on standard
(\Cref{sec:results:headline}). This comparison carries its own confound and we report it alongside the
tension rather than after it: state and option \emph{rendering} alone, with zero training, moves a frozen
backbone's JevBench standard-tier score by a comparable margin to the gap being discussed here -- the same
frozen Qwen3.5-9B checkpoint scores .806 standard under our own rendering and .931 under a different
published system's rendering of the identical items, a 12.5-point swing from rendering choice alone. We
did not sweep rendering for the frozen 14B hard-tier control, so part of its .559-vs-.450 advantage over
our trained model could be a rendering effect rather than a training-vs-no-training effect; we do not have
the controlled comparison to rule this out, and it is a specific, cheap follow-up. The fixes we identified
and applied at 14B -- the long-state fix, DecisionMix v2, the typed heads, and calibration-shaped
checkpoint selection (\Cref{sec:results:mixture}, \Cref{sec:results:kd}) -- narrow this gap on probability
quality (hard-tier Brier moves from .85 to .66, past the frozen model's .60 is close) without closing it
on accuracy (.450 vs.\ .559). Frozen-teacher distillation, the one component of that bundle with its own
matched control, is not the source of the narrowing: removing it (\texttt{tl1b\_nokd}) improves hard-tier
accuracy, \texttt{long\_policy}, and standard-tier Brier over \texttt{tl1b} rather than costing them
(\Cref{sec:results:kd}), so at 14B pushing the model toward its own frozen few-shot judgment is a small net
negative for exactly the items this tension is about, not a fix for it. Two readings are consistent with
the data we have. One is that training sharpens the model's confidence everywhere, including on exactly
the items the training distribution does not cover well, so the trained model is \emph{more confidently
wrong} on hard items than a frozen model that has seen three examples and otherwise falls back on broader
pretraining -- consistent with the Brier-vs-accuracy split (probability-quality metrics move much more
than argmax accuracy under every fix we tried) and with \Cref{sec:analysis:depth}'s argument that the
uncovered items are specifically the compositional ones a single forward pass structurally struggles with.
The other reading is that the fixes we applied at 14B each targeted a different diagnosed defect and none
of them targeted compositional coverage directly, so the tension may simply reflect that the right fix has
not been tried yet. Weak evidence against the tension being architecture-specific: at Qwen3.5-4B, the same recipe reaches
hard-tier accuracy .495 -- above every Qwen3 checkpoint at any size we trained, including 14B -- at a
fraction of the parameters, suggesting the pretraining distribution of the backbone, not the decision
architecture, may set an upper bound the recipe has not yet reached at 14B. We do not have a controlled
experiment isolating pretraining distribution from parameter count to confirm this, and we list it as the
clearest open question this project leaves (\Cref{sec:limitations}).
