Software that asks a language model for a decision needs a probability for each option it supplies,
and a way to say that none of them fit. Typical reads that distribution directly from a pretrained
language model's hidden states, without generating text. It encodes the input document once, reads each
question with its candidate options in a short suffix, and scores every candidate at 71\% of the
network's depth, returning a choice, an ordinal score or a yes/no probability, plus a separate
probability of abstaining. The hard part of building such a model is making the decision depend on the
right inputs. A model can score well while ignoring the question, the evidence or the rubric, or while
shifting with label order and with how many options it is offered, and accuracy shows none of it. We
built Typical around tests that do. A shuffled-question control, $\dq$, shows that question-dependent
knowledge appears only when the candidates are read inside the forward pass, in every configuration we
tested. Training on counterfactual rubric groups makes the model use the rubric, lifting held-out rule families
by 35--40 points over a model never trained on them, and when training truncates long documents,
placing the evidence first adds 11.2 points. A Bernoulli head
makes yes/no answers invariant to label order, and removing the ``none of the above'' option from the candidates, leaving abstention to a separate gate,
cuts MMLU-Pro false abstention from .692 to .003. A decision takes 45\,ms at
1.7B and 60\,ms at 14B, and the 14B model reaches .931 on the JevBench standard tier. We release 1.7B
and 4B models and an inference package.
