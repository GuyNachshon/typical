\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Software increasingly asks a language model to make a decision, not to write a paragraph: route this
ticket to one of forty queues, decide whether this transaction is fraudulent, score the severity of
this alert, choose the next tool call. In these settings the caller ultimately needs a structured
decision -- often a probability distribution over a label set defined at request time -- rather than
free-form text. Existing approaches typically recover that decision through generated answer symbols
or strings, constrained decoding, or candidate log-probabilities, which introduces decoding cost and
a sensitivity to the answer representation that is well documented: option-order and option-symbol
biases, and first-token probabilities that disagree with the text the model would actually generate.

This paper asks one question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a pretrained causal language model be read directly as a decision engine, without
generating an answer at all -- and what has to remain inside its computation for that to work?}
\end{quote}

We break it into three research questions, which organize the rest of the paper.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] Can a decision representation be computed \emph{before} the candidate set is known and
  still preserve question-conditioned language-model capability? (\Cref{sec:repr:blind})
  \item[RQ2] If not, can candidate-aware contextual states expose the decision directly, without
  answer generation? (\Cref{sec:repr:readout,sec:repr:depth,sec:repr:null})
  \item[RQ3] How does such an interface behave under scaling, calibration training, and harder
  compositional decisions? (\Cref{sec:train,sec:boundary})
\end{description}

RQ1 matters because a candidate-blind decision state is the formulation a systems designer wants: one
state, computed once, reusable against any candidate set a runtime enumerates later, with scoring cost
independent of the language model. We tested it and it does not work, in a specific and measurable
way. Under six tested factorizations and distillation objectives, and at both backbone depths we
tapped, a candidate-blind state absorbs a listwise teacher's candidate-set priors and calibration but
not its question-conditioned closed-book capability. That failure is invisible to accuracy: the
students score well above chance. It appears only under a control that replaces the real question with
a shuffled, unrelated one and checks whether the score moves. We make that control a first-class
metric, $\dq$ (\Cref{sec:experiments:deltaq}), and report the negative as load-bearing rather than as
a discarded pilot -- it is the reason the architecture we adopt gives up candidate-independent scoring.

The answer to RQ2 is that the candidates must participate in the language model's own computation, and
that \emph{how} they are represented in the readout matters at the interface level. Typical encodes a
state once and retains its key-value cache; every query against that state is a short causal suffix --
query text and candidate labels together -- attending to the cached prefix and never recomputing it.
A direct readout then scores the terminal decision state against each candidate's own
\emph{contextual} hidden state. Scoring instead against a candidate-blind semantic embedding of the
same option text loses 13 points of knowledge-probe accuracy; scoring against the contextual state
matches a letter-generating interface's question-conditioned signal with roughly a quarter of its
order sensitivity. Recent mechanistic work shows a decoder already knows which multiple-choice option
is correct before it emits the answer symbol \citep{wong2026models}; we build on that finding rather
than restate it, by making the candidate-aware latent representation the deployed output interface and
measuring what is lost when the candidates are removed from the computation.
\Cref{fig:architecture} sketches the resulting decision pass and the typed output contracts on it.

RQ3 gives the boundary. On standard, bounded decisions the recipe generally improves with backbone
size across the tested Qwen3 checkpoints, and soft-target probability quality improves substantially
at fixed capacity once checkpoints are selected on a held-out calibration metric rather than
in-distribution loss. On a harder tier of compositional, multi-step decisions nothing we trained
clears a same-size \emph{frozen} backbone read with three in-context exemplars. We report that
comparison with its own confound: state and option \emph{rendering} alone moves a frozen backbone's
score by a margin comparable to the effect being discussed (\Cref{sec:boundary:rendering}).

\textbf{Contributions.} Three primary, one diagnostic.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A candidate-blind negative result.} Under six tested factorizations and distillation
  objectives, and at both tapped depths, candidate-blind decision states transfer candidate priors and
  calibration but not question-conditioned closed-book capability
  (\Cref{sec:method:blind}, \Cref{sec:repr:blind}). The shuffled-question control that exposes it,
  formalized as $\dq$, separates genuine question dependence from candidate-set priors and is itself a
  reusable diagnostic. We state this as a scoped empirical finding -- under these factorizations,
  objectives, depths, and budget -- not an impossibility theorem.
  \item \textbf{A candidate-aware direct readout.} A cached-prefix decision pass scores a terminal
  decision representation against each candidate's contextual hidden state, recovering the
  question-conditioned signal of a letter-generating interface while reducing order sensitivity and
  removing answer-token generation entirely (\Cref{sec:method:pass}, \Cref{sec:repr:readout}). Typed
  output contracts (categorical, ordinal, Bernoulli) and an abstention gate factored out of the
  candidate softmax are components of that interface (\Cref{sec:method:typed},
  \Cref{sec:repr:null,sec:train:typed}).
  \item \textbf{A capability and scaling boundary.} Across matched scales and frozen controls,
  standard bounded decisions generally improve with scale and soft-target likelihood improves without
  additional capacity; hard compositional decisions remain unresolved and are not better after
  fine-tuning than under frozen few-shot inference (\Cref{sec:train:ladder},
  \Cref{sec:boundary:hard,sec:boundary:frozen}).
  \item \textbf{Diagnosed failure modes} that aggregate accuracy hides: a last-layer tap that discards
  the state, a rendered abstain option that dilutes under softmax competition as the candidate set
  grows, and an evidence-removing truncation defect in long-context training data whose cost we isolate
  from a render-compatibility confound with a $2\times2$ ablation replicated at two scales
  (\Cref{sec:repr:depth}, \Cref{sec:repr:null}, \Cref{sec:boundary:truncation}). We treat these as
  secondary contributions supporting the three claims above.
\end{enumerate}
