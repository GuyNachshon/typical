\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Software increasingly asks a language model to make a decision, not to write a paragraph: route this
ticket to one of forty queues, decide whether this transaction is fraudulent, score the severity of this
alert, choose the next tool call. In every one of these cases the caller does not want text -- it wants a
calibrated probability over a label set it defines at request time, delivered in milliseconds, so that
ordinary code can threshold it. The dominant way of getting this out of a language model today is to
prompt it to \emph{generate} an answer -- a letter, a JSON field, a word -- and then parse that generation
back into a decision. This detour through generation is expensive (one forward pass per candidate, or a
multi-token completion, for every decision), sensitive to the surface form of the answer (option-order and
option-symbol biases are well documented), and poorly calibrated out of the box, because next-token
probabilities were never trained to be probabilities over a request-defined label set.

The gap, concretely: can a pretrained language model be read directly as a decision engine -- one that
consumes a state, a query, and a runtime-defined candidate set, and returns a calibrated distribution over
that set at the cost of a few dozen extra tokens against a cached state -- without generating an answer at
all? And if so, does the resulting model retain the parametric knowledge and evidence-grounded reasoning
the same backbone has when prompted generatively, or does removing the generative interface cost something
structural?

We built and evaluated such a system, which we call \textbf{Typical}, and the central empirical result is
a negative one that shapes everything else in the paper. The most appealing design computes a decision
state \emph{before} the candidates are known -- fully candidate-blind, closer to a dual-encoder -- and
scores candidates against it afterward, which would let a runtime enumerate candidates for free. Under six
tested factorizations and distillation objectives, and at every backbone depth we tried, this design
transfers a listwise teacher's candidate-set priors and calibration but never its question-conditioned
parametric knowledge, a distinction only visible under a control that swaps the real question for a
shuffled, unrelated one and checks that the model's score does not care. We report this as a load-bearing
negative result, not a discarded pilot: it rules out the design that looks obviously right, and it is why
Typical instead renders the candidate set \emph{inside} the same causal suffix as the query. A state is
encoded once and its key-value cache retained; every query against it is a short causal suffix -- query
text and candidates together -- attending to the cached prefix and never recomputing it, so each
candidate's own contextual hidden state carries its position and identity the way it would in an ordinary
multiple-choice prompt. Recent mechanistic work shows a decoder already knows which multiple-choice option
is correct before it emits the answer symbol \citep{wong2026models}; our readout builds on that finding
rather than restating it, turning the latent decision into the output primitive itself and asking what a
direct readout needs to preserve it. The answer turns out to be specific: scoring the terminal decision
state against a candidate-blind semantic embedding of the option text loses 13 points of knowledge-probe
accuracy relative to the letter interface, while scoring it against the option's own \emph{contextual}
hidden state -- carrying the slot identity a semantic embedding cannot -- matches the letter interface
while cutting most of its order sensitivity. \Cref{fig:architecture} sketches the resulting decision pass
and the three typed output primitives (Choice, Score, Noul) built on top of it.

The resulting recipe scales close to monotonically with backbone size on standard, bounded decisions:
\textbf{JevBench} standard-tier accuracy, on the benchmark's public item subset, rises from .694 at 1.7B to
\textbf{.931} at 14B, and .861 at a same-recipe Qwen3.5-4B checkpoint, at 15--60\,ms per decision regardless
of size, because a single decision's cost is dominated by the cached state and a short suffix rather than
backbone depth. Calibration-shaped checkpoint selection takes the model's held-out soft-target negative
log-likelihood from 2.87 -- the worst point on our own size ladder -- to 0.95 at the same backbone size,
with no change to model capacity; a same-backbone frozen-teacher distillation term tested against its own
matched control adds nothing measurable and is mildly negative on the hard tier. Against this, we report
the honest
limit as clearly as the headline numbers: JevBench's hard tier of compositional, multi-step decisions
remains at or near chance for every checkpoint we trained, including our best 14B model, which a
same-size \emph{frozen} backbone with three in-context exemplars and no training at all beats on hard-tier
accuracy -- a comparison we present alongside its own confound, since how the state and options are
rendered moves a frozen backbone's score by a similar margin (\Cref{sec:analysis:tension}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A closed negative result: under six tested factorizations and distillation objectives, and at
  every backbone depth, a candidate-blind decision state absorbs a teacher's candidate priors and
  calibration but never its question-conditioned parametric knowledge, established with a
  question-shuffling control (\Cref{sec:method:blind-closure}, \Cref{sec:results:candidate-blind}). We
  state this as a scoped finding -- under these factorizations, objectives, and budget -- not an
  impossibility result.
  \item A direct decision interface reading calibrated, typed probabilities from a cached language-model
  state with no answer generation, building on evidence that a decoder already encodes the correct
  multiple-choice option before emitting its symbol \citep{wong2026models}: the interface-level pair
  showing a candidate-blind semantic scorer loses 13 points of knowledge-probe accuracy while a readout
  over candidates' own contextual hidden states matches the letter interface with a quarter of its order
  sensitivity (\Cref{sec:method:pass}, \Cref{sec:results:readout}), plus a factored null gate with an exact
  independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives property (\Cref{eq:factored-null}).
  \item An empirical scaling ladder (1.7B--14B, one additional backbone family) with matched frozen
  controls and a calibration-objective isolation (checkpoint selection and target distribution, not
  capacity, is the binding constraint on soft-target probability quality), alongside a hard-tier tension
  that training does not resolve at any size tested, reported together with its own rendering confound
  (\Cref{sec:results:headline}, \Cref{sec:analysis:tension}).
  \item Diagnosis of three failure modes invisible to aggregate accuracy alone -- a last-layer tap, a
  rendered abstain option, and a facts-last truncation bug in long-context training data -- the first two
  isolated with a matched control and the third with an exact measurement plus a matched
  facts-first-vs-facts-last ablation (\Cref{sec:results:readout}, \Cref{sec:results:null},
  \Cref{sec:results:mixture}).
\end{enumerate}
